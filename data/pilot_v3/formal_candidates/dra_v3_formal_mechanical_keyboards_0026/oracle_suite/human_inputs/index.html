公开问题

I type for about six hours a day, and I am trying to work out whether a roughly $40 mechanical keyboard is enough or whether paying toward $150 to $200 buys differences I will actually notice. Listings can use similar broad blue or brown switch labels while pricing whole boards very differently. Please explain where the extra spend can go, what matters for sustained typing and ownership, what mainly changes sound or hobby experience, and how I should set a sensible stopping point.

First, compare the observed price and feature ladder. Treat current catalog prices as snapshots not stable market values, and do not turn seller listings into a performance ranking. Separate layout, connectivity, hot swap, keycaps, case, actuation, display, software, and accessories. Show how those features are allocated across the observed price examples, while making clear that price alone is not a quality score.

Next, separate switch labels from the rest of the keyboard system. Do not treat a shared switch color or family label as complete board equivalence. Map daily typing needs against acoustic and hobby preferences, and separate daily typing fit, quietness, tactile feel, durability, and serviceability from close-up sound preferences. Include needed keys, mouse reach, board height and slope, wrist position, force or tactile response, stabilized-key behavior, connection and firmware behavior, construction, quality control, modification effort, and repairability. Do not assume that higher price guarantees better typing, sound, or lifetime. Treat community posts as scoped experiences not population rates or controlled tests.

Identify which claims require hands-on or longer-term verification. In particular, distinguish what the material can support about layout, construction, durability history, reported feel, reported noise, modification effort, and street prices from what would still need a sustained typing trial, a quiet-room check, or longer ownership evidence.

Finally, set a person-specific purchase boundary without inventing a universal cutoff. State that the material does not establish a universal 150 to 200 dollar cutoff or one objectively best board. Finish with a person-specific purchase boundary and a short hands-on test: choose the least expensive board that passes named daily-use checks or state that no board or universal cutoff is supported. Make the test practical for a switch sample, shop visit, or return window, and say what would make me keep looking rather than pay more automatically.
