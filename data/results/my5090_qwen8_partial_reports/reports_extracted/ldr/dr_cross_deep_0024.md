### 📌 **Historical Evolution of Charging Technology**

The transition from a variety of proprietary charging ports to the widespread adoption of USB-C is a result of both technological advancement and industry standardization. In the early 2000s, each device—phones, laptops, tablets—had its own proprietary charging port and cable. This led to a proliferation of different connectors, which was inconvenient for consumers and inefficient for manufacturers.

The introduction of the **USB (Universal Serial Bus)** standard in the early 2000s was a significant step toward standardization. USB 2.0, introduced in 2004, became the de facto standard for data transfer and charging, and it was widely adopted across consumer electronics. However, USB 2.0 had limitations in terms of power delivery and speed.

The **USB 3.0** standard, introduced in 2008, improved data transfer speeds and power delivery, but it still used the older USB-A connector. The **USB-C** connector, introduced in 2014, was designed to be reversible, smaller, and more versatile. It supports higher power delivery (up to 100W) and faster data transfer speeds (up to 10Gbps with USB 3.2 Gen 2).

The shift to USB-C was accelerated by major tech companies like Apple, Google, and Samsung, who began adopting it in their flagship devices. This led to a **de facto standard** that is now used across a wide range of devices, from smartphones to laptops.

### 🔧 **What Happens Under the Hood: Negotiation Between Devices and Chargers**

When you plug in a USB-C cable, a **negotiation** occurs between the device and the charger to determine the **maximum power and data transfer speed** that can be delivered. This process is known as **USB Power Delivery (USB PD)**.

Here’s how it works:

1. **Handshake Protocol**: When the cable is connected, the device and charger exchange information about their capabilities. This is done through a **handshake protocol** that identifies the supported power levels, data transfer speeds, and other features.

2. **Power Negotiation**: Based on the handshake, the charger and device agree on the **maximum power** that can be delivered. For example, a fast-charging brick might offer up to 30W, while a standard wall adapter might only offer 15W. The device will request the amount of power it needs, and the charger will supply it if it can.

3. **Data Transfer Negotiation**: If the device is also transferring data (e.g., via USB), the two devices will negotiate the **maximum data transfer speed** they can support. This can range from USB 2.0 (480 Mbps) to USB 3.2 Gen 2 (10 Gbps).

4. **Reverse Charging**: USB-C also supports **reverse charging**, which allows a device to charge another device. For example, a smartphone can charge a smartwatch or earbuds via USB-C.

This negotiation ensures that the device receives the **optimal power and data transfer speed** based on its needs and the capabilities of the charger.

### 📦 **Why Cables and Adapters Behave Differently**

Even if two cables look identical, they may behave differently due to several factors:

1. **Cable Quality**: Cheaper cables may use lower-quality materials or thinner wires, which can reduce power delivery and data transfer speeds. High-quality cables use **better conductors** and **reinforced connectors**.

2. **Certifications**: Cables that are **USB-IF certified** (USB Implementers Forum) are guaranteed to meet the standards for power delivery and data transfer. Non-certified cables may not work as intended.

3. **Adapters**: Not all USB-C adapters are created equal. Some may only support **USB 2.0** or **USB 3.0**, while others support **USB PD** and **USB 3.2**. A fast-charging brick may not deliver the same power as a higher-end one.

4. **Device Compatibility**: Even with the same USB-C port, different devices may have **different power requirements**. For example, your daughter’s phone may support **USB PD 3.0**, while your brick only supports **USB PD 2.0**, which could explain the difference in charging speed.
