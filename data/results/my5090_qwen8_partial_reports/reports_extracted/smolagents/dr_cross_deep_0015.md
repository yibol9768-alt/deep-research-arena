The extra cost for more expensive mechanical keyboards typically goes into several components and features that can impact both the durability and the user experience, though not always in a way that directly affects day-to-day typing for average users. Here's a breakdown of where the extra money tends to go and what it might mean for your use case:

---

### **1. Case Quality**
- **Cheaper keyboards** often use **plastic cases** that are functional but may feel less premium, and can be prone to wear over time.
- **More expensive keyboards** often use **aluminum or metal cases**, which are more durable, cooler to the touch, and often more aesthetically pleasing.
- **Impact on typing**: While the material itself doesn’t affect typing feel, a sturdier case can last longer and feel more premium.

---

### **2. Plate or PCB Quality**
- **Cheaper keyboards** may use **lower-quality PCBs** (printed circuit boards), which can be less reliable over time.
- **Higher-end keyboards** use **better PCBs** that are more durable and less prone to failure.
- **Impact on typing**: A better PCB ensures more consistent switch performance and longer lifespan.

---

### **3. Stabilizers**
- **Cheaper keyboards** may use **basic stabilizers**, which can cause **rattle or inconsistent key feel**, especially on larger keys like the spacebar or Enter.
- **More expensive keyboards** often use **higher-quality stabilizers** or even **individual switch mounts**, which reduce noise and improve tactile feedback.
- **Impact on typing**: Better stabilizers lead to a smoother, quieter, and more consistent typing experience.

---

### **4. Switches and Build Quality**
- **Cheaper keyboards** may use **lower-quality switches** or **standard switches** (like Cherry MX Reds or Browns) at a lower cost.
- **More expensive keyboards** often use **premium switches** (like Cherry MX Silent Reds, Kailh Box Reds, or custom switches), which are more durable and offer better actuation feel.
- **Impact on typing**: Premium switches can offer a more consistent and satisfying typing experience, especially for users who type for long periods.

---

### **5. Additional Features**
- **More expensive keyboards** may include features like **RGB lighting**, **customizable profiles**, **anti-ghosting**, or **hot-swappable switches**.
- **Impact on typing**: These features are more about aesthetics or customization and may not directly affect typing performance unless you're using advanced features like macro programming.

---

### **6. Brand and Cachet**
- Some of the extra cost in higher-priced keyboards is due to **brand reputation**, **design**, and **community appeal**.
- **Impact on typing**: Unless you're a collector or enthusiast, this may not add value to your daily use.

---

### **Price Cutoff and Hobby Cachet**
- **Price above which you're paying for hobby cachet**: This is generally around **$150–$200**, where many enthusiast brands (like Kinesis, Ducky, and some custom builds) begin to dominate.
- **Cutoff point**: If you're looking for a keyboard that offers **good value**, **comfort**, and **longevity**, a keyboard in the **$80–$120 range** is often a sweet spot. This range typically includes **quality switches**, **durable cases**, and **reliable build quality** without the premium branding or extra features.

---

### **Recommendation for Your Use Case**
- If you type **6 hours a day**, you'll benefit most from:
  - **Durable switches** (e.g., Cherry MX Reds, Kailh Box Reds).
  - **Good stabilizers** to avoid rattling.
  - **A sturdy case** that can withstand daily use.
- **Avoiding the "hobby cachet" price point** means you can save money while still getting a reliable, comfortable keyboard.

---

### **Conclusion**
The extra money in more expensive mechanical keyboards often goes toward **better materials**, **higher-quality components**, and **premium features**. However, for a user who types 6 hours a day and is looking for a **practical, durable keyboard**, the **$80–$120 range** offers the best balance between cost and quality. Beyond that, the added cost is often more about **brand, aesthetics, and community appeal** rather than tangible improvements in typing performance.
