## C. Technical & Scientific Background (Wiki Sources)

### C1. Polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) Chemistry

[Polytetrafluoroethylene](http://localhost:8090/content/wikipedia_en_all_nopic/Polytetrafluoroethylene) is a synthetic fluoropolymer with carbon-fluorine bonds that provide exceptional non-stick properties. However, it decomposes above 260°C (500°F), releasing perfluorocarbon gases that can cause polymer fume fever in humans. This directly contradicts marketing claims that PFOA-free non-stick is safe at all temperatures.

[Perfluorooctanoic acid](http://localhost:8090/content/wikipedia_en_all_nopic/Perfluorooctanoic_acid) (PFOA) was historically used in PTFE production but has been phased out due to toxicity concerns. However, replacement chemicals (GenX, PFBS) have their own safety concerns. [PFAS litigation and regulations by country](http://localhost:8090/content/wikipedia_en_all_nopic/PFAS_litigation_and_regulations_by_country) documents global restrictions on these chemicals.

### C2. Non-Stick Surface Science

[Non-stick surface](http://localhost:8090/content/wikipedia_en_all_nopic/Non-stick_surface) explains that ceramic coatings (sol-gel) are silica-based and harder than PTFE but more brittle. They have lower thermal stability and are prone to micro-cracking from thermal expansion. This explains why ceramic pans lose non-stick properties faster than PTFE pans.

[Coating](http://localhost:8090/content/wikipedia_en_all_nopic/Coating) technology for cookware includes PTFE, ceramic, and enamel coatings. Each has different durability profiles. [Frying pan](http://localhost:8090/content/wikipedia_en_all_nopic/Frying_pan) history shows that non-stick coatings are a relatively recent innovation.

### C3. Cast Iron Metallurgy

[Cast-iron cookware](http://localhost:8090/content/wikipedia_en_all_nopic/Cast-iron_cookware) is made from iron alloyed with 2-4% carbon. Seasoning creates a polymerized oil layer that provides non-stick properties. Iron leaching occurs when acidic foods react with the metal, but amounts vary based on cooking time, temperature, and food acidity.

[Cookware and bakeware](http://localhost:8090/content/wikipedia_en_all_nopic/Cookware_and_bakeware) discusses different materials' properties. [Induction cooking](http://localhost:8090/content/wikipedia_en_all_nopic/Induction_cooking) notes that cast iron is ferromagnetic and works well with induction cooktops.

### C4. Stainless Steel Composition

[SAE 304 stainless steel](http://localhost:8090/content/wikipedia_en_all_nopic/SAE_304_stainless_steel) contains 18% chromium and 8% nickel. The chromium forms a passive oxide layer that provides corrosion resistance. However, [Austenitic stainless steel](http://localhost:8090/content/wikipedia_en_all_nopic/Austenitic_stainless_steel) explains that acidic conditions can break down this layer.

[List of applications of stainless steel](http://localhost:8090/content/wikipedia_en_all_nopic/List_of_applications_of_stainless_steel) shows that 304 stainless is used in food processing but has limitations. [Chromium](http://localhost:8090/content/wikipedia_en_all_nopic/Chromium) discusses chromium's role in stainless steel's corrosion resistance.

### C5. Anodizing Process

[Anodizing](http://localhost:8090/content/wikipedia_en_all_nopic/Anodizing) is an electrochemical process that thickens the natural oxide layer on aluminum. This layer is porous and can be sealed, but scratches and acidic foods can expose bare aluminum. [Aluminium toxicity](http://localhost:8090/content/wikipedia_en_all_nopic/Aluminium_toxicity) discusses health concerns related to aluminum exposure.

[Titanium](http://localhost:8090/content/wikipedia_en_all_nopic/Titanium) is sometimes used as an alternative to aluminum in cookware. [Silicone](http://localhost:8090/content/wikipedia_en_all_nopic/Silicone) is used in some non-stick coatings but has different properties.

### C6. Thermal Properties

[Heat capacity](http://localhost:8090/content/wikipedia_en_all_nopic/Heat_capacity) and [Thermal conductivity](http://localhost:8090/content/wikipedia_en_all_nopic/Thermal_conductivity) are critical for cookware performance. Aluminum has high thermal conductivity (237 W/mK), while stainless steel has lower conductivity (16 W/mK). Cast iron has moderate conductivity (50 W/mK) but high heat capacity.

[Maillard reaction](http://localhost:8090/content/wikipedia_en_all_nopic/Maillard_reaction) requires temperatures above 140°C (285°F) for browning. Non-stick pans limit browning because they can't reach high enough temperatures safely.

### C7. Health and Safety

[Plastic](http://localhost:8090/content/wikipedia_en_all_nopic/Plastic) and [Microplastics](http://localhost:8090/content/wikipedia_en_all_nopic/Microplastics) are relevant to non-stick coating degradation. ## E. Shopping Rules Cheat-Sheet

1. **Avoid non-stick pans for high-heat cooking** – PTFE coatings degrade above 260°C (500°F), releasing toxic fumes. Use stainless steel or cast iron for searing and browning. [Polytetrafluoroethylene](http://localhost:8090/content/wikipedia_en_all_nopic/Polytetrafluoroethylene)

2. **Replace non-stick pans every 2-3 years** – Even with perfect care, PTFE coatings wear out. Discard immediately if you see peeling, scratching, or flaking. [Non-stick surface](http://localhost:8090/content/wikipedia_en_all_nopic/Non-stick_surface)

3. **Never use metal utensils on non-stick surfaces** – Metal scratches the coating, accelerating degradation and potential chemical release. Use silicone, wood, or plastic utensils. [Coating](http://localhost:8090/content/wikipedia_en_all_nopic/Coating)

4. **Choose stainless steel for acidic foods** – While stainless can leach small amounts of nickel/chromium, it's safer than aluminum or non-stick for tomato sauces and citrus dishes. [SAE 304 stainless steel](http://localhost:8090/content/wikipedia_en_all_nopic/SAE_304_stainless_steel)

5. **Season cast iron properly and avoid acidic foods** – Seasoning creates a natural non-stick
